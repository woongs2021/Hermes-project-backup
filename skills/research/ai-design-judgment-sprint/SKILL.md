# AI Design Judgment Sprint

## When to use

Use when research or a client conversation suggests that AI is changing design work, junior learning, review load, or approval bottlenecks—and Chris needs to translate that signal into a testable OBD, design-leadership, education, or consulting opportunity.

Do **not** use this as proof of market size, repeat purchase, ROI, legal obligation, or causal job displacement. It is a discovery and pilot-validation pattern.

## Core interpretation

Frame the opportunity as a shift from producing more AI output to operating better judgment around it:

- AI can compress routine drafting, variation, and organization work.
- The work that remains valuable includes task selection, context, critique, evidence, revision, exception handling, approval, and responsibility.
- For junior talent, the risk is not only fewer tasks; it is losing the visible learning path from draft → feedback → revision → defensible decision.
- A portfolio should therefore show evidence of judgment: problem framing, alternatives, AI-use boundaries, review criteria, revisions, and approval rationale—not just polished final images.

## First-pilot rule

Start small: **one task + one human approval point + one short settling period**.

Recommended pilot shape:

- **Team:** 5–30 person design, content, or product team.
- **Duration:** two weeks for the intervention; follow with a short review window when possible.
- **Scope:** one real workflow, not company-wide transformation.
- **Decision owner:** begin with the working design/content leader. Bring HR/L&D, security, legal, accessibility, or brand partners in as requirement owners when relevant—not as assumed buyers.

## Choose exactly one costly pain first

Ask the sponsor to choose one primary pain. Do not validate all three at once.

1. **Manager review overload** — too much senior time is spent correcting or approving AI-assisted work.
2. **Rework / approval delay** — AI output reaches review but cycles through unclear edits, waits, or reversals.
3. **Junior judgment gap** — junior staff can create output but cannot explain, critique, or independently defend a recommendation.

Write the choice in this form:

> For [team], reduce [one chosen pain] in [one workflow] without increasing [brand, rights, accessibility, security, or customer] risk.

## Deliverables

Create only the minimum operating artifacts:

1. **Task-selection canvas** — input, desired outcome, AI-suitable subtask, human-only judgment, exceptions, and prohibited use.
2. **Delegation / approval card** — who may delegate, who approves, what evidence is required, and how to stop or escalate.
3. **Review rubric** — quality, user/context fit, brand, accessibility, provenance/rights, factuality where relevant, and a rationale field.
4. **Learning evidence record** — for junior participants: initial direction, alternatives considered, AI contribution, feedback received, revision, final rationale, and approval status.
5. **Before/after review** — a short readout tied to the chosen pain.

## Metrics and decision gate

Measure the selected bottleneck, not generic output volume. Choose one primary metric and at most two safety checks.

Possible primary metrics:

- review turnaround time;
- rework cycles per deliverable;
- exception recovery time;
- manager review minutes;
- share of a workflow a junior can complete and explain independently.

Possible safety checks:

- brand, accessibility, rights, privacy, or factuality issues;
- participant perception that logs are coaching evidence rather than surveillance;
- quality regression in the approved final output.

**Go / revise / stop:**

- **Go** only when the chosen pain improves *and* the team can explain why an output was accepted or rejected.
- **Revise** when the operating artifacts help but the metric is unclear, scope is too broad, or reviewers cannot calibrate the rubric.
- **Stop** when the work creates unmanageable quality/risk issues, cannot be measured, or is perceived as monitoring rather than learning support.

## Evidence and claim discipline

- Separate task exposure from job loss, economic feasibility from technical possibility, and pilot uptake from repeat purchase.
- Treat international labor reports, employer surveys, and small qualitative design studies as signals with different evidentiary strength; do not merge them into one causal claim.
- Never turn an AI regulation page into a legal conclusion. Refer complex requirements to qualified counsel.
- Do not claim that the sprint proves a scalable product, buyer demand, or ROI. Validate those separately through interviews, paid pilots, retention, and renewal evidence.
- Keep participant data and work artifacts private by default. Avoid public logging of identifiable performance records.

## Portfolio-review lens

When evaluating AI-era portfolios, review these six items:

1. What problem and user/context constraint was identified?
2. Which alternatives were considered and why was one direction selected?
3. Where did AI contribute, and what was not delegated?
4. Which rubric or evidence was used to critique the output?
5. What changed after feedback or exception handling?
6. Who approved the result, on what grounds, and what remains uncertain?

This evaluates reasoning and evidence, not visual polish alone. It should not be used as an automated hiring score without validity, bias, privacy, and adverse-impact review.

## Handoff format

For a concise Chris-facing recommendation, return:

- **Observed signal:** fact and source scope.
- **Business hypothesis:** one buyer, one pain, one workflow.
- **Two-week pilot:** deliverables, primary metric, safety checks.
- **Decision gate:** go / revise / stop condition.
- **Open risk:** the most important evidence still missing.

## Common failures

- Starting with “AI transformation” instead of one observable workflow.
- Measuring generated volume rather than rework, delay, judgment quality, or learning evidence.
- Treating AI logs as employee surveillance.
- Calling polished outputs a validated portfolio without process evidence.
- Assuming the same buyer: design leader, HR/L&D, legal, and security often play different roles.
- Presenting an early pilot as market validation or repeatable revenue proof.
